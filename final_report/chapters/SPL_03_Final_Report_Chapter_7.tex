In this chapter, the empirical evidence given by the different components of the proposed adaptive hybrid crowd-counting framework (pretrained classic person detector, head detector fine-tuned on the SCUT-Head dataset, custom four-column multi-column density regression network (Four Column MCNN), the dilated convolution density network style of the CSRNet and a classical detection assisted density estimation procedure) is presented and analysed.

The role played by the Public Transport Crowd Counting Dataset (PTCCD) is quite different, instead of training or fine tuning any of the individual techniques, it is used to test the performance of the proposed, fully populated passenger counting framework under realistic transportation conditions. \subsubsection{SCUT HEAD Dataset}
The fine tuned sparse detector is trained on SCUT-HEAD, a head detection dataset comprising 4{,}329 images with 109{,}067 annotated head bounding boxes~\cite{peng2018scuthead}. The dataset is collected in two parts that differ in imaging condition and scene variability:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Part A:} 2{,}000 images sampled from classroom surveillance video within a single university, with 67{,}318 heads annotated; because classroom scenes recur with limited pose variation, the constituent images were deliberately selected to maximise representative variance. As distributed, Part~A is pre-split into 1{,}100 training, 400 validation, and 500 testing images.
	\item \textbf{Part B:} 2{,}329 images crawled from the internet, with 41{,}749 heads annotated, offering greater visual diversity than Part~A. As distributed, Part~B is pre-split into 1{,}630 training, 466 validation, and 233 testing images.
\end{itemize}

Every annotated head is bounded by an axis aligned box $(x_{\min}, y_{\min}, x_{\max}, y_{\max})$ covering the full head extent, including occluded portions, without extraneous background, following the Pascal VOC annotation convention. In the implementation examined for this study, Part~A and Part~B are pooled into a single combined dataset of 4{,}329 images (2{,}730 for training, 733 for testing) while each image's original split membership is preserved, and a single object class (head) is declared, since the study task is class agnostic head localisation rather than the finer classroom versus internet distinction that motivated the dataset's original two part structure.

\subsubsection{ShanghaiTech Dataset, Part B}
Both learned density estimation techniques are trained and evaluated on Part~B of the ShanghaiTech crowd counting dataset, introduced alongside the multi column convolutional architecture on which one of the two density estimators is based~\cite{Zhu2020}. ShanghaiTech as a whole comprises 1{,}198 images with 329{,}547 annotated head points, split into a dense, internet sourced Part~A (482 images) and a sparser, street-level Part~B (716 images); this study uses Part~B only. Part~B images are all captured at a fixed resolution of $768{\times}1024$ pixels on busy Shanghai streets, officially divided into 400 training and 316 testing images, with per-image counts ranging from approximately 9 to 578 people and a mean of approximately 123.6 people per image, substantially sparser than Part~A (mean $\approx$501 people per image), which was not used in this study. Annotations are provided as a single $(x,y)$ pixel coordinate per head, stored per image in a MATLAB \texttt{.mat} file, rather than as bounding boxes. Both density estimation techniques uses only the official 400-image training subset: a fixed, seeded 90/10 split is drawn from it, giving 360 images for training and holding out the remaining 40 for validation during training; this same 40-image held-out set is subsequently reused as the evaluation set for reported test metrics, so the official 316-image test subset is not used by this technique. During training, this technique additionally applies random-crop augmentation, so the network is fed fixed-size image and density-map crops rather than full frames, whereas the held-out validation/test images are processed at their full $768{\times}1024$ resolution; in both cases the target density map is downsampled by a factor of 8 relative to the input. The other technique uses the official test subset directly as its validation set during training, without a further internal split. Point annotations are not used directly as supervision; both techniques convert them into smoothed density maps prior to training, as described in Section~\ref{sec:dense}.


\subsubsection{Public Transport Crowd Counting Dataset}

The dataset contains a total of 285 images and 285 corresponding annotation files, resulting in a one-to-one correspondence between images and labels. The images represent different passenger configurations and crowd densities, making the dataset suitable for evaluating both sparse and relatively dense passenger-counting conditions.

\section{Interpretation of Results}

\subsection{Classic YOLO: Pretrained Detector}
\label{sec:classic_yolo}

This section reports the experimental results obtained from applying a pretrained, off-the-shelf YOLO object detector to head counting on the public transport crowd counting(285-image) test set used in the preceding experiments. Detections were obtained using the model's default (pretrained) weights with inference parameters \textit{imgsz=1024}, \textit{conf=0.25}, restricted to \textit{classes=[0]} (the COCO ``person'' category). As the pretrained model was optimised to localise whole body person instances rather than heads, a systematic geometric mismatch between predicted and ground truth bounding boxes is expected \textit{a priori}, independent of the detector's underlying ability to identify the presence of individuals. The results reported below are interpreted with this expectation as the analytical baseline.

\subsubsection{Quantitative Detection Based Counting Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:pretrained_yolo_summary} presents the overall detection performance of the pretrained YOLO detector on the 285-image test set. The very low precision, recall, and F1-score at IoU = 0.50 do not necessarily mean that the detector fails to identify people. The main issue is the difference between the annotations and the detector output: the ground truth contains small head only boxes, while the pretrained model produces boxes covering the full person. As a result, a correct person detection may have very little overlap with the corresponding head annotation and therefore be counted as a false positive. The low number of true positives is therefore mainly due to this mismatch between the evaluation annotations and the detector's output format.

The counting error shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pretrained_yolo_actual_predicted} is mainly biased toward underestimation: the detector undercounts in 167 of the 285 test images, overcounts in 98, and is exact in 20. This is likely due to occlusion in crowded scenes, where people overlap with each other and their full body bounding boxes can overlap heavily. As a result, non-maximum suppression may remove some detections even when multiple people are present. In contrast, heads can remain visible even when much of the body is hidden. Therefore, the undercounting is mainly related to the use of whole body detection in crowded scenes rather than a problem specific to this model.

These results show that fine tuning should focus on adapting the detector to the head based annotations rather than simply improving its ability to detect people. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:scut_yolo}, the main issue is the mismatch between whole person predictions and head based ground truth. The largest counting errors occurred in crowded images, where the detector returned fewer than half of the annotated individuals (for example, 10 detections for a ground truth count of 43, and 21 for 54), showing that heavy occlusion can cause severe undercounting as people overlap. This further highlights the need for a head-based detector in crowded scenes.

\subsubsection{Detection Based Counting Performance by Ground Truth Count Range}

The results across the six ground truth count ranges in Table~\ref{tab:pretrained_yolo_range_breakdown} and Figure~\ref{fig:pretrained_yolo_range_metrics} show that absolute counting error generally increases as the crowd becomes larger, from a MAE of 2.6885 in the 1--10 range to 22.4444 in the 51--60 range. However, percentage error is highest in images with fewer people (45.8424\% for 1--10) because even a small counting mistake has a larger effect when the ground truth count is low. Counting accuracy is highest in the moderately populated ranges (21--30 and 31--40), where the crowd is large enough to reduce the effect of individual errors but not so dense that occlusion causes major detection problems.

\subsection{SCUT-Head YOLO: Fine Tuned Head Detector}
\label{sec:scut_yolo}

This section reports the experimental results obtained from training and evaluating a YOLO based object detector on the SCUT Head dataset for head detection, and from using this detector as a head counting mechanism on the same 285-image test set used in the preceding density estimation experiments.

\subsubsection{Training Performance and Convergence}

Detection performance is already relatively high at the first training epoch (precision 0.895, recall 0.833, mAP@50 0.890) because the network uses transfer learning from a COCO pretrained model. Instead of learning detection from the beginning, the model starts with existing detection knowledge and adapts it to the head specific annotations. Therefore, the training process mainly improves the model's existing detection ability to match the smaller head bounding boxes rather than learning object detection from scratch.

Figure~\ref{fig:scut_head_yolo_training_curves} shows the training and validation losses together with the main detection metrics across the 26 training epochs. All three training losses decrease steadily over the run (box loss from 1.834 to 1.561, classification loss from 0.9936 to 0.6306, and distribution focal loss from 1.32 to 1.187), whereas the corresponding validation losses each reach a minimum earlier in training (box and distribution focal loss at epoch 16, classification loss at epoch 22) and subsequently fluctuate within a narrow band without further sustained improvement. Although the run was configured for 50 epochs, training stopped early after 26 epochs, indicating that the early stopping criterion was met before the maximum number of epochs was reached. This indicates that the model achieves most of its generalisation performance before the end of training, with later training mainly improving the fit to the training data.

\subsection{FourColumn MCNN: Density Regression Network}
\label{sec:mcnn}

This section reports the experimental results obtained from training and evaluating the proposed MCNN density estimation model for crowd head counting. The model was trained to learn the relationship between input images and their corresponding density representations, with training and validation performance monitored using loss and mean absolute error (MAE). Although the training schedule was configured for a maximum of 100 epochs, the training process was terminated at epoch 98 as the validation performance had stabilised. The best performing checkpoint was obtained at epoch 68 based on the lowest validation loss and MAE. The results presented below examine the model's training behaviour, validation performance, and final evaluation results, providing an assessment of its effectiveness for density based crowd estimation.

\subsubsection{Training Performance and Convergence}

The training history covers 100 epochs and tracks training and validation loss and MAE, along with the best performing checkpoint. Both training and validation metrics improve substantially during the first $\sim$25 epochs, although validation performance is unstable early in training (for example, the validation MAE rises to 75.07 at epoch 7 before falling again). From approximately epoch 40 onward, all four metrics plateau and remain effectively constant until the final epoch. In contrast to the detection experiment in Section~\ref{sec:scut_yolo}, the MCNN does not employ a task specific pretrained backbone, requiring it to learn relevant features directly from the training data. The resulting progression of training and validation performance is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:mcnn_training_curves}.

\subsection{CSRNet: VGG-16 Based Density Regression Network}

Similar to the proposed MCNN described in Section~\ref{sec:mcnn}, CSRNet estimates a continuous density map and derives the final crowd count by integrating the predicted density rather than explicitly localising individual heads. The two networks therefore represent alternative implementations of the density based counting paradigm and can be compared directly in terms of their training behaviour, aggregate counting accuracy, and performance across different crowd-density ranges.

\subsection{Hybrid Adaptive Counting: SCUT-Head YOLO Combined with MCNN}
\label{sec:hybrid_mcnn}

The fine tunedCUT-Head YOLO detector (Section~\ref{sec:scut_yolo}) performs well in sparse and moderately crowded scenes but increasingly undercounts as crowd density and occlusion increase. \section{Comparison with Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Detection Based Head Counting}

The SCUT-Head dataset was introduced by Peng et al.~\cite{peng2018scuthead} together with a Feature Refine Net and cascaded multi-scale detection architecture specifically designed for small, densely-packed head instances; the original publication does not report results in the precision/recall/mAP@50 format used by the Ultralytics YOLO framework employed here, precluding a direct numerical comparison of detection metrics on identical terms. The general finding of that work, that head detection in dense, small-instance conditions benefits from architectural provisions for small-object and multi-scale detection is broadly consistent with the resolution sensitivity directly observed in Section~\ref{sec:scut_yolo} (a 7.6-point mAP@50 drop when inference resolution was halved), though this study did not test an architecture specifically designed for small-head detection and cannot claim to have reproduced or extended that paper's specific findings.

\subsection{Density-Map Estimation on ShanghaiTech}

The Multi-Column CNN (MCNN) architecture that the Four-Column MCNN implementation extends was introduced by Zhang et al.~\cite{Zhang2016}, reporting a whole-image count MAE of 26.4 on the official ShanghaiTech Part~B \emph{test} set (316 images). CSRNet~\cite{li2018csrnet} later reported a substantially lower MAE of 10.6 on the same official Part~B test set. Neither published figure is directly comparable to the results in Sections~\ref{sec:mcnn}--\ref{sec:csrnet}: both published figures are whole-image count MAE on the official 316-image \emph{test} split, whereas this study's Four-Column MCNN figure (15.20, best epoch) is a per-pixel density MAE on a 40-image internal \emph{validation} split that was never applied to the official test set.
% , and this study's CSRNet figure reflects only 4 of 200 planned training epochs.

% \textbf{No like-for-like MAE comparison against either published benchmark can therefore be responsibly reported for either network in this study.} 

The qualitative pattern that both published works report, substantially lower error on Part~B than on the denser Part~A split, is consistent with, and reinforces, the general density-dependent degradation pattern directly observed for the sparse detection, even though the specific architectures differ.

\subsection{YOLO-Family Detection}

Both the classic and SCUT-Head-fine-tuned detectors are based on the YOLOv8 architecture by Ultralytics~\cite{yolov8_ultralytics}, which follows the single-shot detection framework introduced by Redmon et al.~\cite{redmon2016yolo}. Since the reported COCO benchmarks use a different dataset, task, and evaluation protocol, they are not directly comparable with the head specific SCUT-Head or person-detection results evaluated in this chapter.

\section{Discussion of Findings}
\label{sec:discussion}

The preceding sections evaluated four standalone counting approaches -- a pretrained, off-the-shelf person detector (Section~\ref{sec:classic_yolo}), a head detector fine-tuned on the SCUT-Head dataset (Section~\ref{sec:scut_yolo}), and two density-regression networks, MCNN and CSRNet (Sections~\ref{sec:mcnn} and~\ref{sec:csrnet}) -- together with two adaptive hybrid configurations that fuse the fine-tuned detector with each density-regression network in turn. This section draws these results together and interprets the patterns that recur across methods; their broader implications for deployment, limitations, and future directions are addressed in the concluding chapter (Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}).

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Closes the Geometric Mismatch but Not the Occlusion Gap}
\label{sec:discussion_finetuning}

Comparing the pretrained detector (Table~\ref{tab:pretrained_yolo_summary}) with its fine-tuned counterpart (Table~\ref{tab:scut_head_yolo_summary}) isolates the effect of adapting a general-purpose person detector to head-level annotations. Fine-tuning improves precision at IoU~0.50 from 0.0175 to 0.4541, recall from 0.0150 to 0.4160, and reduces MAE from 7.2351 to 3.3579, confirming that the pretrained model's poor box-level scores in Section~\ref{sec:classic_yolo} were substantially, though not entirely, an artefact of the geometric mismatch between whole-body and head-level bounding boxes rather than a genuine inability to identify individuals -- a distinction already anticipated qualitatively when the pretrained model's count-level correlation with ground truth (Pearson $= 0.8704$) was found to be far stronger than its box-level metrics implied. What fine-tuning does \emph{not} resolve, however, is the underlying occlusion problem: the fine-tuned detector's counting bias remains negative and grows in magnitude across the higher-density ranges (Table~\ref{tab:scut_head_yolo_range_breakdown}), mirroring, in attenuated form, the same density-dependent undercounting observed for the pretrained model (Table~\ref{tab:pretrained_yolo_range_breakdown}). This indicates that occlusion-driven undercounting is a structural limitation of the detection paradigm itself -- a consequence of relying on discrete, non-overlapping instance localisation -- rather than a limitation specific to any one model's training data or weights.

\subsection{Detection and Density Regression Exhibit Complementary Error Structure}
\label{sec:discussion_complementary}

The single clearest pattern to emerge from this chapter is that detection based and density based counting fail in different, largely non-overlapping regions of the crowd-density spectrum. The fine tunedCUT-Head YOLO detector achieves its best relative accuracy in sparse and lightly populated scenes (Table~\ref{tab:scut_head_yolo_range_breakdown}), where individual heads are rarely occluded and can be localised reliably, but its performance -- and, more specifically, its counting bias -- degrades as crowd density increases, with the largest negative bias occurring in the most densely populated range evaluated (51--60 individuals). This behaviour is consistent with the underlying mechanism of detection based counting: non-maximum suppression and increasing inter-person occlusion cause a growing share of true instances to go undetected as density rises, a pattern already visible in exaggerated form for the pretrained, non-fine-tuned detector in Section~\ref{sec:classic_yolo}.

MCNN and CSRNet display the opposite tendency. Both density-regression networks perform poorly in the sparsest range evaluated (1--10 individuals), where mean percentage error exceeds 100\% for MCNN (Table~\ref{tab:mcnn_range_breakdown}) and remains very high for CSRNet (Table~\ref{tab:csrnet_range_breakdown}), before improving substantially and stabilising in the moderate-to-high density ranges. This is consistent with density regression's reliance on learned spatial texture and contextual cues rather than explicit per-instance localisation: in sparse scenes, background clutter and photometric noise can be misattributed as crowd density, producing errors that are large relative to the (small) true count, whereas in denser scenes genuine crowd texture provides a stronger and more consistent signal for the network to exploit.

% Overall, the results show that detection- and density-based methods have complementary strengths that depend on crowd density. This complementarity motivates the hybrid fusion framework evaluated in Sections~\ref{sec:hybrid} and~\ref{sec:hybrid_csrnet}.

\subsection{Hybrid Fusion Reduces Density-Regression Failure but Does Not Surpass the Detector}
\label{sec:discussion_hybrid}

The two hybrid configurations evaluated in this chapter were designed to test whether the complementary error structure identified in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_complementary} could be exploited through simple, rule-based fusion. The results are consistent across both configurations and support two conclusions simultaneously.

First, hybrid fusion is highly effective at neutralising the specific weakness of density regression in sparse and lightly populated scenes. Relative to using MCNN or CSRNet in isolation, fusing either network with the fine-tuned detector reduces aggregate MAE by more than a factor of two (CSRNet: from 10.0025 to 4.3410) and, in the MCNN case, by more than a factor of four (from 18.3891 to 4.4191), while restoring a strongly positive Pearson correlation and coefficient of determination in place of values that were weak or, for MCNN, negative when used alone (Tables~\ref{tab:hybrid_mcnn_component_comparison} and~\ref{tab:hybrid_csrnet_component_comparison}). This demonstrates that a simple, interpretable, threshold-based fusion rule -- requiring no additional training and no learned combination weights -- is sufficient to suppress the great majority of density regression's characteristic sparse-scene overestimation errors.

Second, and more surprisingly, neither hybrid configuration exceeds the accuracy of the fine-tuned detector used on its own. The explanation lies in the asymmetry of the two components' reliability: across the density ranges evaluated in this test set, the fine-tuned detector's counting error, while density-dependent, never becomes as large in absolute or relative terms as either density-regression network's error in the sparse range. Consequently, in the substantial share of images where the two estimators broadly agree and are therefore averaged (55.79\% for the MCNN-based hybrid; 63.86\% for the CSRNet-based hybrid), averaging in a comparatively noisier density-based estimate pulls the fused prediction away from the detector's already more accurate count more often than it corrects for a genuine detector error. In effect, the detector on this test set is simply the stronger of the two estimators across most of the density spectrum evaluated, and unweighted averaging under those conditions is expected to degrade accuracy slightly rather than improve it.

The choice between the two hybrid configurations is nonetheless not immaterial. Selecting CSRNet over MCNN as the density-based component narrows the gap to the detector-only baseline considerably (an MAE increase of 0.5375 over the detector alone, versus 0.6156 for the MCNN-based hybrid; Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_selection}), because CSRNet's sparse-scene predictions are less extreme and therefore less damaging when averaged in. This distinction becomes most consequential in the density-only fallback branch, where the detector produces zero detections and the framework must rely entirely on the density estimate: here the choice of density-regression component determines the difference between a fallback error of a few individuals (CSRNet) and a fallback error of thirty or more (MCNN), on scenes where the true count was as low as three or four. This is arguably the most practically important finding of the hybrid evaluation, since it is precisely in this rare but high-stakes failure mode -- complete detector failure -- that the choice of fallback estimator matters most.

% \label{sec:discussion_implications}

% The practical setting motivating this study -- automated occupancy and crowd estimation in public transport environments such as buses -- typically spans the full range of density conditions evaluated in this chapter, from near-empty vehicles to tightly packed peak-hour crowds, and the specific images drawn from bus interiors that form part of the evaluated test set illustrate this range directly. The density-dependent error patterns identified above therefore have a direct bearing on deployment. A detector-only system would be expected to perform best during off-peak periods but to systematically undercount ridership during the high-density conditions -- crowded boarding, standing passengers, peak-hour services -- that are often of greatest operational interest for capacity planning and overcrowding alerts. Conversely, a density-regression-only system would be least reliable precisely when vehicles are empty or nearly empty, a condition in which accurate counting is comparatively low-stakes but false positives (reporting passengers where none exist) could nonetheless undermine confidence in the system. The adaptive hybrid framework, despite not exceeding the detector's aggregate accuracy in this evaluation, offers a form of risk mitigation that neither standalone approach provides on its own: it converts the density-regression network's largest failure mode (sparse-scene overestimation) into a comparatively minor correction, while retaining detector-level accuracy across the moderate-to-high density conditions that dominate the practical deployment setting. For a public transport operator, a system whose worst-case error is bounded and predictable may be preferable to one that is marginally more accurate on average but occasionally fails catastrophically in either direction, even where, as here, that marginal average advantage favours the simpler detector-only configuration.
