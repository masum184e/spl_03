\mychapter{Results Analysis and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter presents and analyses the empirical evidence produced by the implemented components of the proposed adaptive hybrid crowd-counting framework: a pretrained classic person detector, a head detector fine-tuned on the SCUT-Head dataset, a custom four-column multi-column density regression network (Four Column MDNN), a CSRNet style dilated convolution density network, and a classical detection assisted density estimation procedure.

\subsection{External Evaluation Image Dataset}

The sparse branch, at-scale external evaluation reported in Section~\ref{sec:sparse_external} uses the same 30 image sample of public transport vehicle interior photographs used for the pipeline evaluation reported previously in this study. The images vary considerably in resolution, lighting, viewpoint, and crowd density (visually assessed ground truth counts ranging from 4 to 19 people per image), and were annotated by a single reviewer using point (dot) annotation, a recognised but comparatively noisy ground truth protocol relative to multi annotator consensus. The classic YOLO additionally demonstrates the same underlying pretrained detector on a single image drawn from the identical source image collection, which is used in Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility_case} as a direct, same image cross check between two independent implementations of nominally the same detector.

\section{Interpretation of Results}

\subsection{Classic YOLO: Pretrained Detector, Qualitative Demonstration}
\label{sec:classic_yolo}

The classic YOLO applies an off-the-shelf, COCO-pretrained YOLOv8-nano detector (confidence threshold 0.25, restricted to the ``person'' class, no explicit IoU override supplied so the Ultralytics default of 0.7 applies) to a single image, with no fine-tuning, no held-out test set, and no annotated ground truth captured.

\subsection{SCUT-Head YOLO: Fine-Tuned Head Detector}
\label{sec:scut_yolo}

A YOLOv8-medium model (170 layers, 25{,}856{,}899 parameters, 79.1 GFLOPs at training time; 93 fused layers, 25{,}840{,}339 parameters, 78.7 GFLOPs after inference-time layer fusion) was fine-tuned from COCO-pretrained weights on the SCUT-Head training subset (2,730 images) at an input resolution of 1280\,px, batch size 8, with the AdamW optimiser auto-selected by Ultralytics at a learning rate of 0.002 and momentum 0.9. Training was configured for 50 epochs with early-stopping patience of 10; it stopped after 45 completed epochs (5.265 hours wall-clock), having last improved at epoch 35.

\subsection{CSRNet: Dilated-Convolution Density Network}
\label{sec:csrnet}

The CSRNet implementation follows the original architecture~\cite{li2018csrnet}: a VGG-16 front-end (frozen, truncated after the third block-4 convolution) feeding a back-end of six dilated convolutional layers and a final $1\times1$ convolution producing a single-channel density map, compiled with the Adam optimiser at a learning rate of $1\times10^{-4}$ and trained against a pixel-wise MSE loss with an auxiliary count-MAE metric. Training was configured for 200 epochs with model-checkpointing, learning-rate reduction on plateau, and early-stopping callbacks.

% \label{sec:classical}

% Unlike the two learned density-regression networks, the classical method is a deterministic image-processing procedure that requires no training. 

% The procedure is not a form of edge detection in isolation, but a two-stage density construction: (1) detected head/person centroids are scattered onto a coarse grid (downsampled by a configured factor, with the deployed pipeline's default of 4) and Gaussian-smoothed (with $\sigma$ scaled by the same downsample factor, default 2.0 at the deployed downsample setting), producing a detection-derived density surface whose spatial integral equals the number of input detections; (2) a Canny edge map of the source frame (thresholds 60 and 150) is resized to the same coarse grid, Gaussian-smoothed identically, then rescaled so that its total mass is capped at 15\% of the detection-derived mass, and added to the detection-derived density. This second term is explicitly bounded: it cannot contribute more than an additional 15\% on top of whatever the detector already found, meaning the classical method's output count can never exceed 1.15$\times$ the underlying detector's count, and is mathematically identical to the underlying detection count whenever no image edges are present (\texttt{edge\_density.sum() == 0}).

Its behaviour is analysed here at the level of its implemented mechanism only. Because its output is bounded to at most 15\% above the input detection count, its accuracy on any given image is structurally coupled to, and cannot substantially exceed the accuracy ceiling of, whichever detector supplies its input detections; it functions as a bounded density-smoothing and mild edge-texture correction layer rather than an independent counting method.

\subsection{Detection-Based Head Counting}

The SCUT-Head dataset was introduced by Peng et al.~\cite{peng2018scuthead} together with a Feature Refine Net and cascaded multi-scale detection architecture specifically designed for small, densely-packed head instances; the original publication does not report results in the precision/recall/mAP@50 format used by the Ultralytics YOLO framework employed here, precluding a direct numerical comparison of detection metrics on identical terms. The general finding of that work, that head detection in dense, small-instance conditions benefits from architectural provisions for small-object and multi-scale detection is broadly consistent with the resolution sensitivity directly observed in Section~\ref{sec:scut_yolo} (a 7.6-point mAP@50 drop when inference resolution was halved), though this study did not test an architecture specifically designed for small-head detection and cannot claim to have reproduced or extended that paper's specific findings.

\subsection{Density-Map Estimation on ShanghaiTech}

The Multi-Column CNN (MCNN) architecture that the Four-Column MDNN implementation extends was introduced by Zhang et al.~\cite{Zhang2016}, reporting a whole-image count MAE of 26.4 on the official ShanghaiTech Part~B \emph{test} set (316 images). CSRNet~\cite{li2018csrnet} later reported a substantially lower MAE of 10.6 on the same official Part~B test set. As Table~\ref{tab:fair_comparison} states, neither published figure is directly comparable to the results in Sections~\ref{sec:mdnn}--\ref{sec:csrnet}: both published figures are whole-image count MAE on the official 316-image \emph{test} split, whereas this work's Four-Column MDNN figure (15.20, best epoch) is a per-pixel density MAE on a 40-image internal \emph{validation} split that was never applied to the official test set

% , and this work's CSRNet figure reflects only 4 of 200 planned training epochs.

% \textbf{No like-for-like MAE comparison against either published benchmark can therefore be responsibly reported for either network in this study.} 

The qualitative pattern that both published works report, substantially lower error on Part~B than on the denser Part~A split, is consistent with, and reinforces, the general density-dependent degradation pattern directly observed for the sparse detection branch in Section~\ref{sec:sparse_external}, even though the specific architectures differ.

\subsection{YOLO-Family Detection}

Both the classic and SCUT-Head-fine-tuned detectors in this study build on the YOLOv8 architecture released by Ultralytics~\cite{yolov8_ultralytics}, itself a successor in the single-shot detection lineage established by Redmon et al.~\cite{redmon2016yolo}. As with the SCUT-Head comparison above, Ultralytics' YOLOv8 release documentation reports benchmark figures on the COCO dataset under COCO's own evaluation protocol, which is not the task, dataset, or metric evaluated in this chapter (head-specific detection on SCUT-Head, or person detection on public-transport imagery); no numerical comparison against COCO benchmark figures is therefore drawn.

\section{Discussion of Findings}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Finding 1: Sparse-Scene Counting}

% , and matched ground truth exactly on the one image directly cross-checked in Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility_case}

The evidence gathered across Sections~\ref{sec:classic_yolo}, \ref{sec:scut_yolo}, and \ref{sec:sparse_external} indicates that detection-based counting performs well under sparse-to-moderate crowding: the deployed pipeline's sparse branch achieved an overall MAE of 1.83 across 30 external images, falling to 0.89 within the sparse-only stratum. The SCUT-Head-fine-tuned detector, evaluated separately on its own held-out split, achieved a substantially higher recall (0.96 at default thresholds) than would be expected from a generic person detector applied to small, dense head instances, though this cannot be directly compared to the pretrained detector's performance because the two were never evaluated on the same data.

\subsection{Finding 2: Dense-Scene Counting}

The three approaches intended for dense-scene conditions are, on the available evidence, at markedly different stages of readiness. The Four-Column MDNN completed its full training schedule and shows plausible, if noisy, convergence behaviour (Section~\ref{sec:mdnn}), but has no test-set or external evaluation at all. 

% CSRNet's training was interrupted at 2\% completion, and its own auxiliary count-error metric shows a likely scale anomaly (Section~\ref{sec:csrnet}); 

% its four-epoch trajectory permits no defensible statement about eventual accuracy. 

% (Section~\ref{sec:classical}) 

The classical method is, by construction, bounded to within 15\% of the underlying detector's own count and was not independently evaluated. None of the three dense-scene approaches can currently be said, on this evidence, to out- or under-perform the sparse branch's dense-stratum accuracy (MAE 3.45) reported in Section~\ref{sec:sparse_external}, because no equivalent external evaluation exists for any of them.

% \subsection{Finding 3: Adaptive Methodology}

% As stated in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_results}, no experiment evaluates the adaptive, fused framework end-to-end. The sparse-branch evidence in Section~\ref{sec:sparse_external} — specifically, the roughly fourfold increase in MAE and bias magnitude between sparse and dense strata — constitutes a clear empirical motivation for why an adaptive, density-aware strategy \emph{could} be beneficial, since it demonstrates that a single fixed strategy's accuracy is not uniform across crowd-density conditions. However, motivation is not validation: whether switching to, or fusing with, a density-based estimate actually improves on the sparse branch's dense-stratum performance is an open, untested question on the evidence available, and this chapter does not claim otherwise.

\subsection{Finding 3: External Generalisation}

Generalisation to unseen, externally-sourced imagery was tested only for the sparse branch (Section~\ref{sec:sparse_external}), where accuracy degraded with increasing crowd density — a domain-shift-adjacent finding in the sense that the external images span viewpoints, lighting conditions, and vehicle types not present in the detector's original COCO pretraining data, yet performance remained strong in the majority (sparse) stratum. 

% No generalisation evidence exists for the SCUT-Head detector, Four-Column MDNN, or CSRNet, each of which has been evaluated only on data drawn from the same distribution as its own training/validation split.

% \subsection{Finding 5: Accuracy vs.\ Computational Cost}

This single data point is consistent with the generally expected trade-off between input resolution and detection accuracy in convolutional detectors, but constitutes one paired observation rather than a systematically swept accuracy/speed curve, and should be weighted accordingly.
