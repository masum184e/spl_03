
## Frequently Asked Questions

### About Thesis

<summary>How does the supervisor selection and group formation system work?</summary>

In previous batches, supervisors were assigned randomly. This sometimes resulted in a mismatch between student's research interests and their assigned supervisor's research areas. Similarly, supervisors might receive students who were not particularly interested in their area of research.

To address this issue, a new supervisor selection approach was introduced for our batch. The chairman of the committee provided a Google Form containing the research areas of different teachers, along with randomly assigned teacher codes instead of their actual names.

Each student was required to select three different teachers and their preferred research topics in a specific order of preference. A student could not select the same teacher more than once, even if they selected different topics with that teacher. However, the same research topic could be selected for different teachers.

Based on student's preferences and their previous academic performance, groups were formed. Each group was designed to contain students with different academic levels. Although academic performance was considered during the allocation process, the selection was primarily based on the student's research interests and preference order. A student's first choice was given the highest priority; if their first choice was unavailable, the system considered their second choice, followed by their third choice.

<details>

<summary>What exactly is a thesis?</summary>

A thesis is a structured academic research work in which you investigate a specific problem, question, or idea using a systematic research process.

A thesis usually includes:

* A clearly defined research problem
* A review of existing literature
* Identification of a research gap
* A proposed methodology or approach
* Experiments, analysis, or evaluation
* Results and discussion
* Conclusions and possible future work

For an undergraduate thesis, you are generally not expected to make a groundbreaking discovery. The goal is to demonstrate that you can identify a meaningful problem, study existing work, conduct research systematically, and draw evidence based conclusions.

</details>

<details>

<summary>What is the difference between a thesis and a regular academic project?</summary>

The main difference is the research component.

A regular academic project is often focused on building or implementing something. For example, you might develop a software application to solve a practical problem.

A thesis goes beyond implementation. It asks a research question and attempts to answer it through investigation and evaluation.

For example:

**Academic project:** Build a system that detects heads in crowded environments.

**Thesis:** Investigate how different object detection approaches perform for head detection in highly crowded environments and evaluate their strengths and limitations.

A thesis can include a software system, but the software itself is usually a means of conducting or demonstrating the research, rather than the entire research contribution.

</details>


<details>

<summary>How do I choose a thesis topic?</summary>

Start with areas that genuinely interest you. Then look at recent research papers to understand what problems researchers are currently working on.

A practical process is:

* Choose a broad research area.
* Read review papers and recent research papers.
* Identify problems, limitations, or unanswered questions.
* Narrow the problem to something manageable.
* Check whether you have access to the required data, tools, hardware, and time.
* Discuss potential topics with your supervisor.
* Select a topic that is both interesting and realistically achievable.

Do not choose a topic simply because it sounds impressive. A smaller, well defined research problem is often much better than an ambitious problem that cannot be completed properly.

</details>


<details>

<summary>Do I need to select my thesis topic based on my career field?</summary>

No, it is not mandatory. However, aligning your thesis with your career interests can be beneficial.

For example, if you want to pursue a career in:

- Artificial Intelligence → consider machine learning, computer vision, NLP, etc.
- Software Engineering → consider software systems, testing, architecture, or developer tools.
- Cybersecurity → consider security analysis, intrusion detection, privacy, or authentication.
- Data Science → consider predictive modeling, data mining, or analytics.

Your thesis can also be an opportunity to explore a field before deciding whether you want to pursue it professionally.

</details>


<details>

<summary>How do I know whether my thesis topic is good enough?</summary>

A good thesis topic generally satisfies several conditions:

- It addresses a clear problem.
- The problem is relevant to your field.
- There is existing literature that you can study.
- You can identify a meaningful research gap or limitation.
- The work is feasible within your available time and resources.
- You have a way to evaluate your proposed approach.
- The expected contribution is appropriate for your academic level.

A simple test is to ask: What problem am I solving, why does it matter, what has already been done, and what will my work add?

If you cannot answer these questions clearly, the topic probably needs further refinement.

</details>


<details>

<summary>Can I change my thesis topic after starting?</summary>

Yes, depending on your department and supervisor.

Changing a topic is sometimes necessary when you discover that:

- The required data is unavailable.
- The problem is much larger than expected.
- The proposed methodology is not feasible.
- The research gap is weaker than initially thought.
- The topic does not align well with your available resources.
- A better research direction emerges during the literature review.

However, changing topics repeatedly can waste significant time. Before making a major change, discuss the situation with your supervisor and determine whether you can modify the scope or research question instead of abandoning the entire topic.

</details>

<details>

<summary>How important is the research problem?</summary>

The research problem is one of the most important parts of a thesis. A strong methodology cannot compensate for a poorly defined problem. Your research problem determines:

- What you are investigating
- Why the research is necessary
- What literature you need to study
- What methodology you should use
- What experiments you need to conduct
- How you will evaluate your results

A good thesis usually begins with a clear problem rather than starting with a particular technology or algorithm.

</details>


<details>

<summary>How do I identify a research problem?</summary>

Start by reading existing research in your chosen area.

While reading papers, pay attention to statements such as:

- "However, the existing method..."
- "A limitation of this approach is..."
- "Future work should..."
- "This method performs poorly when..."
- "Few studies have investigated..."
- "The dataset does not represent..."
- "Further research is required..."

These statements can point toward potential research problems.

You can also identify problems from real world situations where existing solutions are inefficient, inaccurate, expensive, inaccessible, or unsuitable for a particular environment.

</details>


<details>

<summary>What is a research gap?</summary>

A research gap is an area where existing research is incomplete, insufficient, inconsistent, or has not adequately addressed a particular question or condition.

A research gap does not necessarily mean that nobody has ever worked on the topic.

For example, researchers may have extensively studied an object-detection method, but perhaps:

- It has not been evaluated in a particular environment.
- Performance decreases in crowded conditions.
- Existing datasets do not represent your target environment.
- Existing methods are computationally expensive.
- Different studies report conflicting results.
- A specific population or geographic area has received little attention.

These can potentially represent research gaps.

</details>


<details>

<summary>How do I find a research gap?</summary>

A useful approach is to read papers systematically rather than randomly.

For each important paper, note:

- What problem does it solve?
- What method does it use?
- What dataset does it use?
- What are its results?
- What are its limitations?
- What does the author identify as future work?
- Under what conditions does the method fail?

Then compare multiple papers. The gap often becomes clearer when you notice that existing approaches consistently leave a particular problem unresolved.

</details>

<details>

<summary>How do I know whether a research gap is actually valid?</summary>

Do not assume that a statement in one paper automatically represents a valid research gap. Verify it by checking multiple sources.

Ask:

* Is the problem genuinely unresolved?
* Have newer papers already addressed it?
* Is the problem relevant to the research community?
* Can the problem be investigated with available resources?
* Can you measure whether your proposed solution improves the situation?
* Is there sufficient literature to establish the current state of the field?

A good literature review should demonstrate why the gap exists, rather than simply claiming that it exists.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Can I work on a topic that has already been researched?</summary>

Yes. In fact, most undergraduate thesis are based on existing research areas. You do not need to find a completely untouched topic.

Instead, you can investigate an existing problem from a different perspective, under different conditions, using different data, or with an improved methodology.

Research is often about extending, testing, comparing, adapting, or improving existing knowledge.

</details>


<details>

<summary>How much novelty is required in an undergraduate thesis?</summary>

The required level of novelty depends on your supervisor, and research area. Generally, an undergraduate thesis does not need the level of novelty expected from a major doctoral research contribution.

Your contribution might involve:

* Improving an existing method
* Comparing existing approaches
* Applying a method to a new environment
* Creating or evaluating a dataset
* Combining existing techniques
* Optimizing an existing approach
* Conducting a systematic evaluation
* Investigating a previously under-studied problem

The important thing is that you can clearly explain what your work contributes beyond the existing literature.

</details>


<details>

<summary>Do I need to invent a completely new algorithm?</summary>

No.

Inventing a completely new algorithm is not a requirement for most undergraduate theses. You can conduct meaningful research by studying and improving existing algorithms or by investigating how existing methods behave under conditions that have not been adequately studied.

For example, comparing several existing algorithms on a new dataset and explaining their strengths, weaknesses, and performance can constitute meaningful research when the study is properly designed.

<details>

<summary>What should I do if I lose motivation during the thesis?</summary>

Losing motivation during a long research project is normal. A thesis can feel overwhelming because the final goal is far away and the research process often contains uncertainty.

When this happens:

* Break the work into very small tasks.
* Set short-term goals instead of focusing only on the final thesis.
* Keep track of what you have already completed.
* Discuss problems with your supervisor instead of struggling alone.
* Take reasonable breaks when you are mentally exhausted.
* Avoid comparing your progress with other students.
* Remember that research often involves failed experiments and unexpected results.

Most importantly, do not wait for motivation before working. Try to maintain a small, consistent amount of progress even on unproductive days.

A thesis is rarely completed through a few highly productive days. It is usually completed through consistent progress over a long period of time.

### Literature Review

<details>

<summary>What is review paper? Should you include it in your literature review?</summary>

A review paper is a type of academic article that summarizes, analyzes, and synthesizes existing research on a specific topic instead of presenting new experimental results. Think of it as a “big picture” paper—it gathers many studies and explains trends, debates, gaps, and conclusions in the field.

**What a review paper does**

- Collects results from many previous studies
- Compares and contrasts findings
- Identifies patterns, disagreements, or gaps
- Suggests future research directions

**You should not include it in your literature review.**

- Your literature review should mainly include primary research articles (original studies)
- Review papers are secondary sources, so overusing them can make your work less strong academically

Start with review papers to build understanding, use their references to find key original studies

</details>

<details> 

<summary>What is a literature review?</summary>

A literature review is a systematic and critical examination of previous research related to your research topic.

It is not simply a collection of paper summaries. A good literature review:

* Identifies what researchers already know.
* Compares different studies and their findings.
* Shows where researchers agree or disagree.
* Identifies theories, methods, datasets, and approaches that have been used.
* Evaluates the strengths and weaknesses of previous research.
* Identifies limitations, inconsistencies, or unanswered questions.
* Builds an argument for why your research is necessary.

For example, if your thesis is about machine learning based disease prediction, your literature review might examine:

What algorithms have been used → what datasets were used → how researchers evaluated them → what results they obtained → what limitations remain → what your study will address.

The final goal is to move from "What has been studied?" to "What still needs to be studied, and why?"

</details> 

<details>

<summary>How is a literature review different from simply reading papers?</summary>

Reading papers is an activity. A literature review is the analysis and synthesis that results from that activity.

When you simply read papers, you might understand each paper individually:

* Paper A used CNN and achieved 94% accuracy.
* Paper B used SVM and achieved 91% accuracy.
* Paper C used Random Forest and achieved 92% accuracy.

A literature review goes further:

> Most previous studies have focused on CNN based approaches and report high predictive performance. However, comparisons are difficult because the studies use different datasets and evaluation metrics. Furthermore, relatively few studies evaluate model robustness across independent datasets.

The second version is a literature review because it connects and evaluates multiple studies.

Think of it this way:

> Reading papers = collecting knowledge

> Literature review = organizing, comparing, evaluating, and synthesizing that knowledge to establish the research context and gap

</details>

<details>

<summary>Where should I search for research papers?</summary>

Use several sources rather than relying on one database. Major academic search platforms:

* Google Scholar — excellent starting point for broad searches.
* Scopus — useful for comprehensive literature searching and citation analysis.
* Web of Science — strong multidisciplinary citation database.
* PubMed — particularly useful for medicine, biology, and health sciences.
* IEEE Xplore — important for electrical engineering, computer science, electronics, and related fields.
* ACM Digital Library — particularly useful for computer science and software-related research.
* ScienceDirect — large collection of scientific and technical publications.
* SpringerLink — broad collection across science, engineering, medicine, and social sciences.
* Wiley Online Library — useful across many academic disciplines.

Also search the official websites of major journals and conferences in your field.

A practical strategy is:

```
Google Scholar
      ↓
Identify Important Papers / Authors / Journals
      ↓
Search Relevant Databases
      ↓
Follow References and Citations
      ↓
Identify the Strongest and Most Relevant Studies
```

</details> <details> <summary>15. How do I convert limitations into a research gap?</summary>

This is one of the most important steps in thesis research.

A limitation is a weakness or boundary of an existing study.

A research gap is an important area where existing knowledge is insufficient, inconsistent, or incomplete.

For example:

Previous study:

Used Dataset A with 500 samples.

Limitation:

The dataset is relatively small.

But:

"The dataset is small."

isn't automatically a strong research gap.

You need to establish why the limitation matters.

For example:

Several studies demonstrate strong performance using small, controlled datasets. However, it remains unclear whether these models maintain comparable performance on larger and more diverse real-world datasets. This study therefore investigates...

The logical chain is:

Previous research → limitation → why the limitation matters → what remains unknown → research question → your contribution

A good research gap should answer:

What do we not know yet?

rather than simply:

What did previous researchers do poorly?

The safest approach is to distinguish clearly between:

Your ideas and other researchers' ideas.

When taking notes, avoid copying sentences directly into your normal notes. Instead:

Read the paper.
Close it or move away from the text.
Write what you understood in your own words.
Record the citation immediately.
Compare your wording with the original.

Also remember:

Paraphrasing does not mean changing a few words.

Original:

Researchers found that the proposed method significantly improved classification performance.

Weak paraphrase:

Researchers discovered that the suggested method significantly increased classification performance.

That's essentially the same sentence with synonyms.

A genuine paraphrase changes the structure and expression while preserving the meaning, and still requires a citation.

And importantly, citation does not make copied text automatically acceptable. If you reproduce exact wording, quotation rules may apply.

Sufficient validation.

### Research Methodology

<details>

<summary>What is methodology?</summary>

Methodology is the systematic plan you use to answer your research question.

It explains how you will conduct your research, including:

* What data you will collect.
* Where the data will come from.
* How the data will be prepared.
* What model or algorithm you will use.
* How you will train the model.
* How you will evaluate it.
* What experiments you will perform.
* How you will compare your results.
* What limitations and assumptions apply.

For a computer-vision thesis, your methodology might look like:

```
Research Problem
       ↓
Dataset Collection
       ↓
Annotation
       ↓
Preprocessing
       ↓
Data Augmentation
       ↓
Train / Validation / Test Split
       ↓
Model Selection
       ↓
Training
       ↓
Evaluation
       ↓
Comparison
       ↓
Error Analysis
```

A methodology should be detailed enough that another researcher could understand exactly how your study was conducted and potentially reproduce it.

</details>

<details>

<summary>How do I decide which methodology to use?</summary>

Start with your research question, not with your favorite algorithm.

Ask:

* What exactly am I trying to determine?
* What type of data do I have?
* What type of output do I need?
* What methods have previous researchers used?
* What are the limitations of those methods?
* What resources do I have—data, GPU, time, software, expertise?
* How will I evaluate the result?

For example:

Research question: Can objects of type X be automatically detected in images?

Your methodology could involve:

```
Image Dataset
     ↓
Annotation
     ↓
Object Detection Model
     ↓
Training
     ↓
Validation
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
mAP / Precision / Recall
     ↓
Comparison with Existing Approaches
```

The methodology should be justified by the research objective and evidence from previous literature, rather than:

> I chose YOLO because YOLO is popular.

A stronger justification is:

> YOLO was selected because the research requires object localization with relatively fast inference, and previous studies have demonstrated its suitability for similar detection tasks.

</details> <details> <summary>3. How do I choose between traditional computer vision and deep learning?</summary>

It depends on the complexity of your problem and the characteristics of your images.

Traditional computer vision

Examples include:

Thresholding
Edge detection
Contour analysis
Histogram-based methods
SIFT/SURF-type features
HOG
Hand-designed color/shape features
Classical classifiers such as SVM or Random Forest

These approaches can work well when the objects have predictable visual characteristics and the environment is controlled.

For example, if an object always has a specific color, shape, and background, traditional methods may be sufficient.

Deep learning

Deep-learning approaches are generally more suitable when:

Images have complex backgrounds.
Objects vary in appearance.
Lighting changes.
Objects appear at different scales.
Objects can be partially occluded.
Manual feature engineering is difficult.
You have enough labeled training data.

For object detection, deep-learning models such as YOLO are particularly useful.

A good thesis strategy

Don't automatically assume deep learning is better.

If appropriate, compare:

Traditional baseline → Deep-learning model → Performance comparison

This can make your methodology stronger because you demonstrate why your chosen approach is appropriate.

</details> <details> <summary>4. Why did I choose YOLO for object detection?</summary>

YOLO—You Only Look Once—is a family of one-stage object-detection models.

A reasonable justification for choosing YOLO is that it performs object detection and localization in a single model, making it suitable when you need to identify objects and their bounding boxes efficiently.

Possible reasons for selecting YOLO include:

Fast inference.
End-to-end object detection.
Good balance between speed and accuracy.
Ability to detect multiple objects in an image.
Availability of pretrained models.
Relatively accessible training workflows.
Strong ecosystem and tooling.
Suitability for real-time or near-real-time applications.

However, your thesis should connect the choice to your specific problem.

For example:

"YOLO was selected because the proposed system requires detection of multiple target objects with accurate localization while maintaining computational efficiency."

Then support the justification with relevant literature and experiments.

Don't claim that YOLO is universally the "best" detector. Its suitability depends on your dataset, hardware, object sizes, evaluation requirements, and competing methods.

</details> <details> <summary>5. Which YOLO version should I use?</summary>

There is no universally correct YOLO version.

The important point is to choose a specific implementation and version, because "YOLO" refers to a large family of models developed by different research groups and organizations.

When choosing a version, consider:

Accuracy.
Inference speed.
Model size.
Hardware requirements.
Training support.

Whether the implementation is actively maintained.
Whether it supports the detection task you need.

YOLO family/version

Select 2–3 plausible models → train/evaluate them under comparable conditions → choose based on your research requirements.

</details> <details> <summary>6. Should I use a pretrained model or train from scratch?</summary>

For most thesis projects with a relatively limited custom dataset, start with a pretrained model.

Training from scratch requires substantially more data and computational resources and may be unnecessary.

A pretrained object-detection model has already learned general visual features from a large dataset. You can adapt those learned representations to your specific task.

Pretrained model

Advantages:

Faster convergence.
Usually better performance with limited data.
Less computationally expensive.
Requires less training time.
Practical for custom datasets.
Training from scratch

May be appropriate when:

You have a very large dataset.
Your images are extremely different from common pretraining data.
You have sufficient computational resources.
Your research specifically investigates training from scratch.

For most student projects, a reasonable starting point is:

Pretrained model → transfer learning → fine-tuning on your dataset

</details> <details> <summary>7. What is transfer learning?</summary>

Transfer learning means using knowledge learned from one task or dataset to help solve another related task.

For example, a model may initially be trained on a large general image dataset. It learns useful visual representations such as:

Edges
Textures
Shapes
Patterns
Object structures

You then adapt that model to your own object-detection dataset.

Conceptually:

Large general dataset → pretrained model → your dataset → adapted model

The advantage is that your model doesn't need to learn every visual feature from zero.

This is particularly useful when your custom dataset is relatively small.

</details> <details> <summary>8. What is fine-tuning?</summary>

Fine-tuning is the process of continuing training of a pretrained model on your specific dataset so that its parameters adapt to your task.

For example:

Pretrained YOLO model → train using your annotated images → customized YOLO model

During fine-tuning, the model adjusts its learned parameters based on your data.

Transfer learning is the broader strategy.

Fine-tuning is one of the ways you implement that strategy.

In practice, you may initially freeze some layers and train the task-specific components, then progressively allow more of the network to adapt if necessary.

</details> <details> <summary>9. How do I choose the model architecture?</summary>

Choose architecture based on the requirements of the research problem, not simply popularity.

Consider:

Accuracy

How accurately must objects be detected?

Speed

Does the system need real-time inference?

Hardware

Do you have:

CPU only?
Consumer GPU?
High-end GPU?
Cloud computing?
Dataset

Consider:

Number of images.
Number of classes.
Object sizes.
Image resolution.
Background complexity.
Degree of occlusion.
Deployment

Will the model run on:

Desktop?
Server?
Mobile device?
Embedded system?
Edge device?
Reproducibility

Can you obtain and document the model, weights, code, and training configuration?

A useful thesis experiment is to compare model variants under the same dataset and evaluation protocol.

For example:

Small model vs. medium model vs. larger model

Then compare:

mAP + precision + recall + inference time + model size

This provides an evidence-based reason for your final architecture choice.

</details> <details> <summary>10. What preprocessing should I apply to my dataset?</summary>

Preprocessing should be purpose-driven. Don't apply every possible preprocessing technique just because it is available.

Common preprocessing steps include:

Image validation

Check for:

Corrupted images.
Duplicate images.
Unreadable files.
Incorrect formats.
Missing annotations.
Resize

Images may need to be resized to the input resolution expected by your model.

Normalization

Pixel values may be transformed into an appropriate numerical range according to the model/training pipeline.

Color handling

Ensure consistent color representation and channel ordering.

Annotation validation

Check whether:

Bounding boxes are correctly positioned.
Class labels are correct.
Boxes are within image boundaries.
Objects haven't been accidentally omitted.
Data cleaning

Remove or investigate:

Extremely poor-quality images.
Irrelevant images.
Duplicates.
Incorrectly labeled samples.

Be careful with aggressive preprocessing. If your real-world images contain different lighting, colors, or backgrounds, preprocessing that artificially makes the images too clean may reduce real-world performance.

</details> <details> <summary>11. What data augmentation techniques should I use?</summary>

Data augmentation creates modified versions of training images to increase variation and improve generalization.

Possible techniques include:

Horizontal flipping.
Small rotations.
Scaling.
Cropping.
Translation.
Changes in brightness.
Contrast changes.
Saturation changes.
Noise.
Blur.
Perspective transformations.

For object detection, augmentation must preserve the correct relationship between the image and bounding boxes. A transformation that moves an object must also correctly transform its annotation.

Don't use augmentation blindly.

For example, vertical flipping may be unrealistic if your objects normally have a fixed orientation.

The question should be:

What variations will the model encounter in the real world?

Then design augmentation to represent those variations.

For example, if deployment images have different lighting conditions:

brightness/contrast augmentation may be useful.

If objects appear at different scales:

scaling and cropping may be useful.

</details> <details> <summary>12. How do I prevent overfitting?</summary>

Overfitting occurs when the model learns the training data too specifically and performs poorly on unseen data.

For example:

Training performance = 98%
Validation performance = 72%

This may indicate a generalization problem.

Strategies include:

More diverse training data.
Data augmentation.
Transfer learning.
Appropriate regularization.
Early stopping when appropriate.
Reducing unnecessary model complexity.
Careful hyperparameter selection.
Avoiding excessive training.
Proper train/validation/test separation.
Removing data leakage.
Evaluating on genuinely unseen data.

For computer vision, data diversity is often extremely important.

A dataset with 10,000 nearly identical images may be less useful than a smaller dataset containing diverse:

Backgrounds
Lighting conditions
Object sizes
Viewing angles
Object appearances
Real-world environments

Also inspect training and validation curves. If training loss continues improving while validation performance stops improving or deteriorates, investigate possible overfitting.

</details> <details> <summary>13. How should I divide my dataset into training, validation, and testing sets?</summary>

A common starting point is something like:

70% training / 15% validation / 15% testing

or:

80% training / 10% validation / 10% testing

There is no universal mandatory ratio.

The critical requirement is that the test set remains unseen during model development.

For object detection, also ensure the splits are representative of the real problem.

For example, don't put almost all images from one location in training and all images from another location in testing unless you intentionally want to test cross-location generalization.

Most importantly, avoid data leakage.

If you have several nearly identical frames from the same video, randomly putting neighboring frames into training and testing can make performance appear much better than it really is.

Where appropriate, split at the level of:

Subject
Patient
Location
Video sequence
Acquisition session

rather than blindly splitting individual images.

</details> <details> <summary>14. What is the difference between training, validation, and testing data?</summary>
Training data

Used to learn the model parameters.

The model sees these examples repeatedly during training.

Validation data

Used during model development and selection.

You can use it to:

Compare models.
Tune hyperparameters.
Determine when training should stop.
Select model configurations.
Testing data

Used for the final evaluation.

Ideally, you don't use test performance to repeatedly make development decisions.

A simple analogy:

Training = studying

Validation = practice exam

Testing = final exam

If you repeatedly change your model based on test performance, the test set is no longer a truly independent final evaluation.

It depends on your research question.

You can use:

Existing public datasets.
Institutional datasets.
Data collected by yourself.
Data collected from real-world environments.
A combination of sources, where ethically and legally appropriate.

Creating your own dataset can be valuable because it allows you to address a specific research gap.

How images were collected.

Sampling procedure.
Ethical considerations.
Permissions and licensing.
Annotation procedure.
</details> <details> <summary>16. How many images do I need?</summary>

There is no universal number.

The required dataset size depends on:

Number of classes.
Difficulty of the detection problem.
Number of objects per image.
Variation in object appearance.
Image quality.
Background complexity.
Object size.
Occlusion.
Model complexity.
Transfer learning.
Desired performance.
Deployment conditions.

For example, detecting one visually distinctive object in a controlled environment may require much less data than detecting several visually similar objects under uncontrolled conditions.

Instead of asking:

"Do I have 5,000 images?"

ask:

"Do I have enough diverse examples for every important condition the model will encounter?"

You should also examine the number of instances per class, not only the number of images.

One image can contain many objects, so:

1,000 images ≠ necessarily 1,000 training examples.

</details> <details> <summary>17. How do I determine whether my dataset is large enough?</summary>

There is no single statistical threshold for object-detection datasets.

Use an empirical approach.

Train your model with progressively increasing amounts of training data, if feasible.

For example:

25% → 50% → 75% → 100%

Then evaluate performance on the same validation set.

If performance continues improving substantially as you add data, more data may be useful.

If performance begins to plateau, you may be approaching a point where adding more similar data provides diminishing returns.

Also inspect:

Performance per class.
Precision.
Recall.
mAP.
False positives.
False negatives.
Performance under different conditions.
Performance on rare cases.

A dataset can be "large" numerically but still inadequate because it lacks important variation.

</details> <details> <summary>18. What should I do if my dataset is imbalanced?</summary>

Dataset imbalance occurs when some classes have substantially more examples than others.

For example:

Class A: 5,000 objects
Class B: 500 objects
Class C: 50 objects

The model may learn the majority classes better than the minority classes.

Possible approaches include:

Collect more minority-class data

Often the best solution when practical.

Targeted augmentation

Create realistic variations of underrepresented classes.

Sampling strategies

Increase the frequency with which minority examples contribute to training, depending on your training framework.

Review class definitions

Sometimes apparent imbalance results from unnecessarily fine-grained classes.

Evaluate per class

Don't report only overall performance.

Report metrics such as:

Precision by class.
Recall by class.
AP by class.
mAP.

This reveals whether the model is failing on minority classes.

Be cautious about simply duplicating minority images many times. If the model repeatedly sees the same examples, it can overfit them.

</details> <details> <summary>19. How do I annotate images?</summary>

For object detection, annotation normally means drawing a bounding box around each target object and assigning the appropriate class label.

For example:

Image → Object → Bounding box → Class label

Common annotation tools include:

CVAT
Label Studio
Roboflow Annotate
LabelImg
Other tools compatible with your chosen dataset format

The exact tool matters less than annotation quality and consistency.

Establish annotation rules first

Before annotating thousands of images, define:

What counts as an object?
Which classes exist?
What should happen with partially visible objects?
How should heavily occluded objects be labeled?
Should truncated objects be included?
How should overlapping objects be handled?
What is the minimum object size?
How should ambiguous cases be treated?

Then create a small annotation guideline.

Perform quality control

After annotation:

Inspect random samples.
Check missing objects.
Check incorrect classes.
Check inaccurate boxes.
Check overlapping objects.
Check annotation consistency between annotators.

If multiple people annotate your dataset, give them the same guidelines and measure agreement or conduct systematic quality checks.

Annotation format

Your model may require a particular format, such as:

Class ID + normalized bounding-box coordinates

or another format depending on the implementation.

Always verify that your annotation conversion is correct before training.

A technically excellent model cannot compensate for systematically incorrect labels.
